01

The business

Rolls-Royce makes the engines and power systems that sit at the safety-critical heart of aircraft, warships, submarines and data centres. Civil Aerospace builds and, crucially, services the big engines on widebody jets, earning for decades after the sale through long-term service agreements, the "power-by-the-hour" model where revenue rises with engine flying hours. Defence supplies military engines, naval propulsion and submarine nuclear reactors, including for the AUKUS programme. Power Systems, the mtu brand, makes the diesel and gas systems increasingly in demand for data-centre backup power. And a fourth leg, small modular nuclear reactors, is moving from concept to order book.

The moat is the installed base and the certification barrier: once a Rolls engine is on a wing, it generates high-margin aftermarket revenue for thirty years, and the regulatory and engineering hurdles to displacing it are immense. Three years ago this was a near-bankrupt company. Today, under Tufan Erginbilgiç's transformation, it is one of the highest-quality industrials in the FTSE. The only open question is what you should pay for it.

02

The turnaround and operating drivers

The historical report’s central argument remains valuable. Rolls-Royce is paid for decades through long-term service agreements tied to large-engine flying hours and maintenance events. The installed base, certification barriers and shop-visit economics create a formidable moat, but cash conversion fluctuates with shop visits, contract balances and spare-parts availability.

From £2.5bn underlying operating profit in 2024 to £3.5bn in FY2025, the turnaround has moved from promises to measurable economics. The H1 2026 release then raised the bar: £2.53bn first-half underlying operating profit, a 22.5% margin and £1.96bn free cash flow. Civil Aerospace, Defence and Power Systems all improved, reducing dependence on one turnaround lever.

Installed base versus the aerospace cycle

The younger Trent fleet can support a long aftermarket runway, but flying hours do not translate mechanically into cash each quarter. Higher shop visits, spares inflation and the timing of long-term-service-agreement balances can interrupt conversion. The 2026 company outlook expects large-engine flying hours towards the lower end of 115–120% of 2019 levels, not an uninterrupted acceleration.

Financial quality and cash conversion

Higher reported profit now has cash behind it. In H1 2026 the group generated £1.96bn of free cash flow, versus £1.58bn a year earlier, and returned to 22.0% adjusted return on capital. The group reported £2.14bn net cash at 30 June. Those numbers support the investment case more directly than a headline statutory earnings multiple, which can be distorted by hedging and tax accounting.

Earnings-quality test

Continue separating statutory and underlying profit, tax credits, hedge remeasurements, contract catch-ups and operating cash generation. The £497m of H1 2026 Civil Aerospace contractual margin improvements deserves particular scrutiny: management itself expects a smaller contribution in H2. Valuation should not capitalise one-off benefits as perpetual growth.

04

Management and capital allocation

Tufan Erginbilgiç, chief executive since January 2023, arrived describing a “burning platform”. The evidence since then is stronger than a typical turnaround narrative: repriced contracts, higher divisional margins, a repaired balance sheet and cash returns to shareholders. Underlying H1 2026 profit rose 46%, supported by stronger Civil Aerospace contract economics, Defence aftermarket and Power Systems demand.

The harder phase is ahead. The company has committed £7–9bn of buybacks across 2026–28, including £2.5bn intended for 2026, while investing in engine service capacity, data-centre power and longer-dated SMR development. The test is whether buybacks are executed at sensible valuations without impairing reinvestment opportunities. The easy self-help phase cannot recur indefinitely.

Valuation in context and intrinsic value

The June note placed house fair value around 1,310p with a 1,000p bear case, 1,300p base case and 1,650p bull case. That baseline was formed before the July guidance upgrade. It should remain visible as a record, but not be quietly recycled as an October valuation. Against the more recent 1,538p reference quote, the June blend would suggest ~15% overvaluation; that conclusion is not our refreshed valuation, because earnings power has improved.

They are not adopted here. The original June scenario is preserved as a historical baseline; fresh formal price bands need a reconciled three-to-five-year shareholder-return assessment.

07

Safran event, and the case both ways

Safran reported Q3 2026 organic growth of 13.4% but slower propulsion aftermarket momentum. Its spare-parts aftermarket was still up 18.7%, yet investors worried about deceleration into 2027 amid airline cost pressure. The initial sell-off is evidence of expectation risk, not proof that Rolls-Royce’s installed base is suffering an equivalent deterioration.

Why the read-across may be excessive. Rolls-Royce is concentrated in widebody long-haul engines and long-term servicing; Safran has a different engine mix and aftermarket profile. The H1 Rolls figures showed 4% growth in large-engine flying hours and improved LTSA economics, with July guidance raised.

Why it may be real. Both businesses ultimately depend on flight activity, airline economics, spare-parts demand and constrained engine-shop capacity. Higher shop visit costs or fewer flying hours could depress Rolls cash conversion even while accounting earnings remain strong.

Variant perception

The central question has evolved from “is the turnaround genuine?” to “how durable are the new margins and cash flows across an airline cycle?” A sector sell-off creates opportunity only if it widens the discount to fairly reassessed economics. The strongest bull case is a younger installed base, improving contract quality and diversified growth in Defence and Power Systems; the strongest bear case is that exceptional recent contract gains are over-capitalised in the share price.

08

Peers, currency and portfolio fit

Safran and MTU are the closest operating comparators for civil engine exposure, but product mix and contract structures differ materially. GE Aerospace illustrates the premium investors award large installed bases and aftermarket profit, while BAE Systems is a useful in-library alternative for defence exposure without the same civil flying-hour sensitivity. Comparative multiples are only useful when normalised for these differences.

Rolls is quoted in pence and reports in sterling, but civil aftermarket revenue is materially dollar-exposed while much of the cost base is sterling; multi-year hedging reduces, not eliminates, the effect. A direct holding adds aerospace cycle and defence exposure rather than diversifying away from industrial and geopolitical risks.

What would change the call

  • Upgrade conviction: 2026 cash delivery at or above guidance, sustained LTSA profitability without repeat catch-ups, stable shop capacity and evidence that the aftermarket slowdown at Safran is not transferring to the Trent fleet.
  • Downgrade: engine flying-hour weakness, prolonged shop-visit constraints, cash conversion lagging profit, or deteriorating contract economics that make the current multiple unsustainable.
  • Reassess action bands: update formal price thresholds only after refreshed forward owner cash flow and downside economics reconcile with the original June scenarios. A price decline alone is insufficient.
11

Investment judgement

A transformed business with increasingly credible cash economics. Verdict: Watch / hold existing exposure, reassess add-price after the latest upgrade and the Safran read-across. Do not confuse a sector scare with a proven valuation opportunity.

Rolls-Royce enters the next stage with stronger margins, rising cash flow, net cash and three operating divisions generating higher profit. The July guidance upgrade strengthens the intrinsic-value case relative to the June report. But a 1,538p reference quote implies demanding forward earnings expectations, and the Safran episode is a warning about the fragility of aftermarket sentiment. Preserve the long-term compounder thesis, examine service-contract economics and cycle sensitivity, then set fresh buy and trim levels against independently modelled prospective returns. The historical 1,150p/1,550p bands remain documented, not automatically actionable.
