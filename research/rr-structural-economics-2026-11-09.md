# Rolls-Royce: structural economics versus recovery and management execution

**Date:** 9 November 2026 · **Evidence cutoff:** before the scheduled **12 November 2026** trading update  
**Purpose:** Explain *why* the group earns far more today than it did before the transformation, how much of the improvement may endure, and which claims remain unproven. This is **not** a new fair value or buy order. Read with the [existing RR thesis](../RR_investment_thesis.html) and the [flying-hours note](rr-engine-hours-2026-11-09.md) **if and when that separately proposed note is merged**. The source links below are usable independently.

## Bottom line

**Judgement:** There is strong evidence of a **structurally improved business**, especially in Civil Aerospace commercial contracts, durability/time-on-wing and Power Systems product economics, alongside deleveraging. But **H1 2026 operating margin is not a safe run-rate**: reported Civil Aerospace profit contains material catch-up and provision-release effects recognised as existing long-duration contract estimates improve. These are economically meaningful revisions, but the same profit uplift cannot be repeated indefinitely. The decisive test is *sustainable per-engine-hour owner cash after all shop visits and investment*, not reported flying-hour growth or a single year's adjusted margin.

**Management role:** Tufan Erginbilgiç and his team accelerated commercial renegotiation, cost control, project selectivity and accountability. Management is a **catalyst** for modifying the economics of a long-lived installed fleet, but it has not created the underlying installed engine base, customer switching costs or aviation aftermarket. A higher profit floor is credible; its exact level is **not yet proven across a downturn and a full maintenance cycle**.

Nonetheless, the later improvement cannot be explained merely by restoring flying hours to 2019.

## 2. What changed, and will it endure?

| Economic driver | Historical deficiency | Evidence of improvement | Persistence assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Long-term service agreement (LTSA) pricing/terms** | Engine maker took lifetime maintenance obligations, with unfavourable rates or overly optimistic cost estimates | Higher normalised flying-hour rates; commercial optimisation; renegotiations and contract profitability improvements | **High potential durability** for contracts already repriced; persistence of future renewals and new contract terms still to test |
| **Time on wing / engine durability** | Premature removals and repair costs damaged lifetime economics | Fleet improvements and stated 2028 aim to carry more EFH with fewer annual shop visits | **Structural if delivered**, but engineering and supply-chain execution remains the critical risk |
| **Original equipment (OE) profitability** | New widebody engines often sold at a loss to secure aftermarket annuities; 2019 average widebody OE loss ~£1.2m | 2028 objective for Trent XWB installed engine deliveries to reach breakeven or positive economics | **Plausible structural improvement**, not fully demonstrated yet |
| **Scale of installed fleet / flying hours** | Pandemic utilisation collapse and fleet disruption | 2025 17.0m large LTSA EFH; H1 2026 8.4m, +4% YoY and 113% of 2019; independent RR fleet tracker October +3.0% YoY | **Secular installed-fleet runway + cyclical travel risk**; 2019-hour comparisons subject to engine/contract-mix changes |
| **Power Systems** | Lower-margin mix and fragmented operations | 2025 17.4% margin; H1 2026 20.3%, with strong data-centre/power-generation volumes and improved mix | **Partly structural**, some cyclical AI-capex and product mix; this is real current business, unlike SMR speculation |
| **Defence** | Procurement/political exposure and project economics | 2025 14.4% margin; H1 2026 21.0%, with aftermarket and submarine benefit | **Mixed**: installed services and programme duration help, but unusually strong H1 margin may not recur |
| **Financial resilience / capital discipline** | 2022 net debt ~£3.25bn; weak capacity to absorb shocks | 2025 net cash £1.895bn; H1 2026 net cash £2.136bn, cash dividends/buybacks resumed | **Structural if sustained**; buyback price and cash reinvestment still matter |

**Critical distinction:** Long-life engine contracts may lock in better economics, but large maintenance obligations remain. The advantage survives *only if* actual life-cycle cost estimates and rates continue to validate the updated margins.

## 3. The catch-up accounting trap

H1 2026 Civil Aerospace underlying operating profit was **£1.567bn** on **£6.186bn** of revenue: **25.3% margin**. Within this, Rolls-Royce disclosed **£497m of net contractual and operational improvements**, versus **£288m** in H1 2025. The 2026 figure consists of **£372m contract catch-ups** and **£125m onerous-contract provision releases**.

**Arithmetic sensitivity, not restated adjusted earnings:** £1.567bn minus £497m = **£1.070bn**, or **17.3% of H1 Civil Aerospace revenue**. This is *not* an independently verified sustainable margin: some prior-year catch-ups would recur through normal reassessment, and some genuinely repriced lifetime contracts will support future service profitability. Removing every adjustment is deliberately severe; retaining all £497m as a recurring half-year flow is equally misleading.

Of the **£374m YoY increase** in H1 2026 Civil Aerospace operating profit, **£209m** was the increase in recognised net contract/operational improvements (**£497m less £288m**). So **about 56%** of the *incremental* reported profit improvement came from a larger such contribution. The remaining ~£165m increase points to trading growth, mix, commercial and cost improvements, subject to other changes and line-item reconciliation. **This is a decomposition of disclosed figures, not a conclusion that only 44% of underlying economics improved.**

Management explicitly said it expects **a lower contribution from contractual margin improvements in H2 2026**. The 2028 Civil Aerospace **21–23% margin target** itself is below H1 2026's **25.3%**. Both statements warn against projecting the H1 margin indefinitely.

## 4. Flying hours to owner cash: the full test

**Demand:** RR H1 2026 large engine EFH **+4%** YoY; independent BNP/Cirium RR fleet tracker **+3.0%** in October 2026. These are not interchangeable with IATA global passenger RPK.

**Receipts:** H1 2026 LTSA invoiced flying-hour receipts **£3.4bn**, up from **£3.0bn** in H1 2025, benefiting from activity and contract rates.

**Costs:** Large-engine **major shop visits 294** in H1 2026, versus **217** a year earlier. Higher shop work consumes cash now even where the underlying contracts are profitable over their full lives.

**Balance-sheet cash timing:** Net Civil Aerospace LTSA balance growth, after risk/revenue-sharing partners, was **£86m** H1 2026 versus **£472m** H1 2025. A growing LTSA credit balance represents billed amounts against *future services owed*, not a pot of unconstrained distributable cash. Higher long-term cash margins require future maintenance and contractual costs to stay below assumptions.

**2028 operating promise:** management expects **130–140% of 2019 large-engine EFH** and **1,300–1,400 total shop visits** against **1,480–1,550 expected for 2026**, with a **£0.8–1.2bn annual net LTSA balance growth** target. **[Inference]** If delivered on a comparable fleet, fewer visits alongside greater flying hours could be powerful economic evidence of improved durability. But mix, timing and deferred-shop backlog can also produce fewer visits temporarily, so verify **time on wing, removals, costs per event and backlog**.

**Stress question:** Would 2028–2030 cash still be strong if EFH growth slowed, catch-up gains faded, shop visits ran above target and interest in data-centre generation normalised? This should be the investor's counterfactual, not a single stylised 'aviation downturn' narrative.

## 5. The CEO versus the company

**Historical franchise:** switching costs, certified engine technology, decades of LTSA service, manufacturer-installed base, repair know-how, long-cycle defence programmes and MTU Power Systems services all predate the current management. They are moat-like *business* characteristics.

**Management-enabled changes:** repricing old and new LTSAs, renegotiating risk sharing, revamping cost control, improving engine durability programmes, prioritising return on capital and improving OE terms require real competence. Most are **not automatically reversible** on a CEO departure, but future execution and culture determine whether the benefits persist.

**Evidence threshold for an enduring higher floor:** through several years, contract rates stay strong **without unusually large catch-up credits**; shop-visit economics remain healthy when visits peak; working capital and LTSA balances do not reverse sharply; cash profit remains strong across aviation cycles; Power Systems can maintain margins without exceptional AI infrastructure spending.

**Provisional judgement:** The 2022 margin was clearly not the business's economic ceiling. **A durable improvement is more likely than a wholesale reversion**, but a defensible floor (say 15%, 18% or 20% group margin) cannot yet be derived from observed full-cycle cash data. Treat any such percentage as a **scenario**, not a fact.

## 6. Valuation implications, without false precision

- At **~1,286p intraday 9 November** (delayed quote) and ~£106bn equity value (indicative), the H1-upgraded **FY2026 FCF guidance £3.8–4.0bn** represents **~3.6–3.8%** equity FCF yield.
- The **2028 FCF target £5.0–5.3bn** implies **~4.7–5.0%** on that *same equity value*, if achieved. This is not discounted, not a present-value estimate, and excludes share count changes and further owner claims.
- Those numbers imply a **strong business is still being priced as one capable of meaningful future cash generation**. The case to add must come from a credible path to higher sustainable per-share cash flows **beyond** the stated 2028 target, or a sufficiently reduced purchase price, not the old June 1,310p fair-value level.
- A £4–5k 'meaningful' position is a **sizing guide**, not the reason to buy. The existing RR position (~£1.9k in the October snapshot) is small; only incremental expected return versus diversified fund exposure justifies a change.

**Current investment judgement:** credible staged-add candidate worthy of valuation review, **not** a confirmed Buy based on the 2022–2026 margin expansion alone. Preserve index hedge; no forced sale or automatic fund reduction before identifying the superior opportunity.

## 7. Thursday 12 November decision gate

At the announced trading update check in this order:

1. **FY26 FCF**: £3.8–4.0bn retained or changed, explain any change in cash taxes, investment and LTSA balance rather than just group profit.
2. **Civil Aerospace**: large-engine EFH versus independent tracker; invoice rates; shop visits, time on wing, supply constraints and mix.
3. **Adjusted-contract gains**: any incremental catch-ups or releases, and whether normalised trading profit is growing.
4. **Power Systems**: actual data-centre and governmental power orders versus revenue and margin conversion; don't treat SMR development as cash-generative today.
5. **Long-run outlook**: 2028 £5.0–5.3bn FCF and 18–20% group margin credible without extrapolating anomalous H1 performance.

**Green:** normalised civil profit and cash improve with fewer life-cycle maintenance costs; **amber:** reported profit rises mostly via further accounting catch-ups while cash underperforms; **red:** EFH slowdown together with margin/durability/shop bottlenecks or FCF cuts. The market's response can be informative but is not a substitute for economics.
